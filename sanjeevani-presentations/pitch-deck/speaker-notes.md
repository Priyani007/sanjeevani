# Sanjeevani Pitch Deck — Speaker Notes

Target duration: **5–7 minutes** (12 content slides + cover + sources)

---

## Slide 1 — Cover (0:00–0:10)
**Key message:** First impression — serious product, respectful tone.

Say nothing during the cover. Let it breathe for 5–8 seconds.
Then: *"I want to start with a question."*

**Transition:** Direct to Slide 2.

---

## Slide 2 — The Opening Question (0:10–0:40)
**Say:**

> "Imagine someone sitting next to you suddenly collapses. Emergency services have been called. You are on the line with a dispatcher and help is a few minutes away. What would you do while you wait?

> Most of us know the answer in theory. We have seen CPR in films. We may have attended an awareness session. But have we actually practised it — on a mannequin, with corrections, until we felt confident?

> For most people in India, the honest answer is no."

**Key message:** Distinguish knowing what to do from having practised it.

**Transition:** *"That gap — between knowing and having practised — is exactly what Sanjeevani is designed to close."*

---

## Slide 3 — Why This Matters in India (0:40–1:30)
**Say:**

> "There are four barriers that limit access to repeatable, practical training.

> First: instructor availability. Organized hands-on training requires qualified instructors, a scheduled venue, and participants who can attend. That is not available everywhere or at every time.

> Second: passive learning. Videos, classroom presentations, posters — these explain the concept well, but they cannot tell you that your hands are in the wrong position or that your rate is too slow.

> Third: equipment cost. Feedback-enabled CPR mannequins — the kind that can tell you whether your compressions are deep enough or your rate is correct — are a meaningful investment for any institution. Procurement, maintenance, storage, and scheduling all add to that cost.

> Fourth: repetition. A single session is not enough. Skills decay. Without regular opportunities to practice and receive correction, confidence and technique decline over time."

**Key message:** Four specific, honest barriers — not a general claim that India has no training.

**Do NOT say:** "Nobody in India has access to training."

**Transition:** *"Let me show you why watching — on its own — may not be enough."*

---

## Slide 4 — Watching Is Not Practising (1:30–2:10)
**Say:**

> "A video is excellent at explaining the sequence of steps. It is less useful at telling you, right now, that your wrists are bent the wrong way — or that you stopped too soon.

> Interactive practice does something different. The learner acts. The system observes what it can. When there is enough evidence, it provides corrective guidance. The learner retries. Over time, the record of those attempts shows where improvement happened and where more practice is needed.

> Videos and classroom teaching are valuable — I am not dismissing them. The argument is about the additional dimension of individual, corrective, repeated practice that they cannot easily provide on their own."

**Key message:** Passive is valuable; interactive adds the feedback dimension.

**Transition:** *"The traditional tool that provides that feedback is physical training equipment."*

---

## Slide 5 — Equipment Economics (2:10–2:55)
**Say:**

> "Physical CPR mannequins range from basic anatomical models with no sensors to advanced feedback-enabled systems with compression-depth measurement and digital session reports.

> The advanced systems are genuinely useful — they can measure what a camera cannot. Compression depth, for example.

> The economic question is not whether mannequins are valuable. They are. The question is whether every learner can access one every time they want to practice. The answer, for most institutions, is no.

> So how do we make selected parts of the practice experience accessible without requiring equipment for every revision session?"

**Key message:** Respect for physical equipment; honest about the access gap.

**Do NOT say:** All mannequins cost ₹30,000–₹1,00,000. Every mannequin is imported.

**Transition:** *"Established training programmes in the United States have been asking this question for years."*

---

## Slide 6 — What the West Teaches Us (2:55–3:40)
**Say:**

> "The American Heart Association and American Red Cross have long recognized that hands-on practice is central to CPR skill retention. Their courses emphasize manikin practice, supervised repetition, and real-time feedback — not just classroom explanation.

> The research on bystander CPR outcomes is consistent: quality of compressions matters. And quality comes from having practiced. Not watched.

> The opportunity in India is to adapt that delivery model to local constraints — using the smartphone that most people already carry as a complementary tool between formal sessions."

**Key message:** Sanjeevani is aligned with established learning principles, not reinventing them.

**Transition:** *"So — what is Sanjeevani, specifically?"*

---

## Slide 7 — Introducing Sanjeevani (3:40–4:20)
**Say:**

> "Sanjeevani is an Android emergency-skills training platform. It runs entirely on the phone — no internet required, no account needed. 

> It provides guided step-by-step training, camera-assisted feedback for actions the phone can reliably observe, voice-directed coaching, and progress records built from genuine training events.

> Importantly, Sanjeevani does not claim to replace a qualified instructor or a training mannequin. It aims to make selected parts of the practice experience accessible between formal sessions — to reduce the gap between workshops, not to eliminate the workshop."

**Key message:** Honest framing as a complement, not a replacement.

**Transition:** *"The engine behind all of that is what we call the learning loop."*

---

## Slide 8 — The Learning Loop (4:20–5:10)
**Say:**

> "The learning loop has seven stages. The app instructs. It observes available input — camera, voice, or button. It validates whether there is enough evidence to assess the action. When there is, it provides corrective guidance. The learner rechecks and retries. The result is recorded. And over time, that history drives improvement.

> The diamond in the middle — 'enough evidence?' — is the honest heart of the engineering. When the camera cannot verify a placement, the app shows that uncertainty. It does not pretend the camera saw something it could not measure. The learner can retry — or tap to continue manually, which is recorded as manual, not as verified."

**Key message:** The inconclusive path is a feature, not a limitation.

**Transition:** *"That loop powers three training modules."*

---

## Slide 9 — The Three Modules (5:10–5:50)
**Say:**

> "CPR walks the learner through the full cardiac arrest workflow — from calling 112, to patient positioning, hand placement with directional AR correction, and live compression coaching at 110 BPM. The phone counts compression cycles and estimates rate. It cannot measure depth — we are honest about that.

> Stroke uses the FAST protocol — Face, Arms, Speech, Time. The camera looks for face asymmetry and arm drift. Speech assessment requires a tap because that is the one thing a phone camera genuinely cannot evaluate.

> Allergic reaction guides through a 6-phase protocol — lay flat, raise legs, EpiPen preparation, injection simulation, and monitoring. The camera shows a target marker on the correct injection site.

> Three more modules — AED, Choking, Recovery Position — are listed as coming soon."

**Key message:** Each module is honest about what camera can and cannot verify.

**Transition:** *"Let me show you this working."*

## Slide 11 — From Errors to Practice (7:20–8:00)
**Say (if time allows — can be condensed or skipped in a 5-min pitch):**

> "One important engineering decision: we do not count every camera frame as a separate error. If your hands are misplaced for three seconds, that is one episode, not ninety events. Analytics come from real, deduplicated training data — not inflated counts.

> And manual continuation — when the learner taps past a step the camera could not verify — is stored as manual. It is never promoted to 'verified success'."

---

## Slide 12 — Affordability (8:00–8:35)
**Say:**

> "A compatible Android phone gives access to supported digital practice without requiring equipment for every revision session. Schools, colleges, community programs, workplaces — anywhere people want to revise between supervised sessions.

> We are not claiming this replaces physical training. We are claiming it lowers the barrier to revision — making the gap between workshops shorter and more productive."

---

## Slide 13 — Validation (8:35–9:10)
**Say:**

> "We need to be honest: we do not yet have controlled study results. These are proposed metrics and a proposed pilot design.

> Success should not be measured by downloads or completion counts alone. It should be measured by whether learners actually perform better on a qualified instructor's assessment after using the app. That is the pilot we want to run."

---

## Slide 14 — Closing (9:10–9:40)
Read the closing statement slowly. Pause before the final line.

> *"The goal is not to replace the training room. It is to make practice possible beyond it."*

## Q&A Preparation

**Q: Can this replace CPR certification?**
A: No. It is a practice tool, not a certification programme. Formal certification requires supervised assessment by a qualified instructor.

**Q: What if the camera tracking fails?**
A: The learner sees an inconclusive state and can tap to continue manually. Manual continuation is recorded as manual — it is never promoted to verified. Retry and tap-button fallbacks are always available.

**Q: How accurate is the compression count?**
A: It counts torso-relative hand motion cycles — an estimate, not a clinical measurement. Depth cannot be measured from a phone camera; we are explicit about that in the session report.

**Q: What happens to user data?**
A: Everything is on-device. SharedPreferences only. No internet, no account, no server.

**Q: Is this medically approved?**
A: Not yet. Clinical protocol review by qualified medical professionals is required before formal deployment. The content aligns with Resuscitation Council UK 2021 and AHA 2020 guidelines, but this is not a substitute for that review.
