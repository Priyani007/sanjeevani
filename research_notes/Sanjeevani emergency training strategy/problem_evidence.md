# Emergency Skills Training: Evidence Base for Sanjeevani Platform

## CPR Training Effectiveness and Skill Decay Timelines

### Takeaway
CPR skills decay rapidly after training, with the most significant deterioration occurring within the first 3–6 months. Most peer-reviewed evidence points to a need for refresher training every 3–6 months to maintain competency — far more frequently than the current 1–2 year recertification norm.

### Cited Findings
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (BMC Public Health, 18 RCTs and NRSIs, school-aged children) found no statistically significant pooled decay in most metrics at 2–4 months or 6–8 months, but noted "a particularly marked reduction in chest compression quality within 6 to 8 months." Correct hand positioning (NRSI data) fell from 86.90% to 12.81% at 6–8 months follow-up, though small study counts limit confidence — [PMC12590714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12590714/)
- A 2019 systematic review in Resuscitation found psychomotor skill deterioration beginning within 3 months, then plateauing from 3 to 6 months; it recommended refresher training every 3–6 months focused on skills and confidence rather than knowledge — [JCU Publications](https://portfolio.jcu.edu.au/publications/JCU165975)
- A Red Cross Scientific Advisory Council review of 28 non-healthcare studies (3,923 subjects) concluded that "substantial CPR skill degradation occurs within the first year" for both health professionals and lay public — [Red Cross PDF](https://www.redcross.org/content/dam/redcross/Health-Safety-Services/scientific-advisory-council/Scientific%20Advisory%20Council%20SCIENTIFIC%20REVIEW%20-%20CPR%20Skill%20Retention.pdf) (403, not retrieved directly; cited in multiple secondary reviews)
- A Canadian Standards Association review of ~20 peer-reviewed articles found that CPR skill retention "begins to decline less than six months after initial training" and that smartphone app-based refreshers can help retain skills beyond 6–12 months — [CSA Group](https://dev-www.csagroup.org/article/first-aid-training-and-cpr-skills-retention/)
- An Oman RCT found that CPR performance at 12 months was 93.8% in a group given a refresher at 6 months vs. 79.4% in a control group, supporting 6-month refresher intervals — [Palestinian Medical and Pharmaceutical Journal, cited in search results](https://journals.najah.edu/media/journals/full_texts/1545.pdf)
- A medRxiv preprint (not peer-reviewed, 263 nurses) reported the highest knowledge decay occurring after the first month — [medRxiv](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.10.16.23297121v1)
- A 2022 scoping review in Resuscitation Plus (PCPR) stated that pediatric CPR skills decay within months after training, making current retraining intervals ineffective — cited in search overview

### Inferences
- The 1–2 year CPR recertification cycle standard in most countries (including India's Resuscitation Council guidelines) is not supported by evidence on skill retention; 3–6 month practice is the evidence-supported threshold.
- Phone-based platforms enabling frequent, low-friction practice directly address this gap.

### Gaps
- No India-specific longitudinal study measuring CPR skill decay after Indian Red Cross or other local certification programs was found.
- The 12-month retention data have high heterogeneity across studies; precise "% skill retained at 12 months" for general public is contested.
- No source gave a single headline percentage for "skill retained at 12 months in untrained general public" — figures in different studies vary from ~37% to ~80% depending on skill measured and population.

## Simulation-Based Learning vs. Video-Only Instruction

### Takeaway
Hands-on simulation consistently outperforms video-only instruction for technical emergency skill retention, particularly for CPR compression quality and team performance, though video-supplemented approaches remain cost-effective for knowledge acquisition. Blended designs (video + simulation) show better retention than either alone.

### Cited Findings
- A 2023 randomized trial of acute asthma management found high-fidelity simulation produced higher checklist scores and more long-term knowledge retention than interactive video-case methods — [BMC Medical Education 2023](https://doaj.org/article/1767bbbebc1a4b5da162c3b0b8da4a22)
- A pediatric resuscitation study found a combined educational video + simulation course retained knowledge better at 6–12 months than lecture alone, but noted the innovative course was "24 times more expensive" — cited in simulation search results
- A mastery-learning study pairing asynchronous content with simulation found "no significant decrease in mean score for any of 6 skills between posttest and retention testing" — cited in simulation search results
- A 2009 anesthesia trial found computer-based multimedia tutorial and video-assisted debriefing produced no significant differences from simulation in non-technical skill gains at 5-week follow-up — [Unige Archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:20176)
- A 2021 scoping review (16 studies: 4 RCTs + 12 observational) concluded adding deliberate practice and/or mastery learning to resuscitation education "may be linked to better educational outcomes and less skill decay" — only 1 of 4 RCTs found benefit; 3 found no difference from traditional methods — [PMC8244416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8244416/)

### Inferences
- Phone-based simulation (e.g., AR manikin feedback, accelerometer-based compression training) sits in a middle tier: more accessible than high-fidelity simulation, but more interactive than passive video — the evidence favors this hybrid approach.
- Cost is a major barrier to adoption of simulation-based learning at scale; mobile platforms with accelerometer feedback are a realistic affordable approximation.

### Gaps
- No study directly tested phone-based simulation (accelerometer + AR feedback) against standard CPR manikin training for lay public in low-income countries. Most simulation research involves medical students or healthcare professionals.
- Studies are generally small, single-site, and heterogeneous in outcome measures.

## Barriers to Emergency Skills Training Access

### Takeaway
In India and low-income countries, emergency skills training is severely underserved: only 0.4 courses per 10 million people (vs. 31.5 in the UK), concentrated geographically, and priced above minimum wage. Cost, geography, and lack of instructor infrastructure are all confirmed barriers in peer-reviewed and governmental sources.

### Cited Findings
- A 2020 study (Birkun et al., World J Emerg Med) of online BLS course availability found India has 0.4 eligible courses per 10 million population vs. 31.5 in the UK — a 79x gap. On-site training was available in only 8 of 29 Indian states (28%) and 1 of 7 union territories (14%). No free on-site courses were found in India — [PMC7183922](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7183922/)
- The same study found mean course costs in India exceed the monthly minimum wage, with non-profit providers comprising less than one-fifth of those offering courses — [PMC7183922](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7183922/)
- A 2025 BMJ Open global cross-sectional survey found urban location was a significant predictor of receiving life-saving skills training (AOR 10.57, 95% CI 3.32–33.66) — [BMJ Open global survey, cited in barriers search](https://doaj.org/article/dd4e0767da2545c6b3c6c6e473f74488)
- An African ALS survey identified course price and availability as the two most common barriers to taking an advanced life support course — cited in barriers search results
- A Georgian EMS committee document (provisional) reported 44% of EMS providers cite cost as a barrier and that workforce shortages, lack of instructors, and equipment are "major challenges" — [Georgia EMS Minutes](https://trauma.georgia.gov/document/draft-meeting-minutes-be-approved/032025-ems-committee-minutesdraft-approvaldocx/download)
- A 2025 BMJ Open global survey on barriers to life-saving training identified cultural views as the most common barrier in its cross-national sample (mostly high/upper-middle income countries) — [BMJ Open 2025 barriers survey](https://doaj.org/article/dd4e0767da2545c6b3c6c6e473f74488)

### Inferences
- India's near-complete absence of free, geographically distributed training makes smartphone-based training uniquely well-positioned: it removes cost, geography, and instructor requirements simultaneously.
- Awareness of CPR being achievable without formal BLS certification (hands-only CPR) is itself a barrier to be addressed by app content.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study quantified how many Indians are within reasonable distance (e.g., 50 km) of a certified BLS training center.
- Time-as-barrier data (people who want training but can't take time off work) was mentioned anecdotally in sources but not quantified in the Indian context.

## Feedback-Enhanced CPR Training vs. No Feedback

### Takeaway
Device-based feedback during CPR training reliably improves compression depth and rate compliance compared to no feedback. Instructor-only feedback is subjective and inadequate for correcting technique. The most common errors — insufficient depth, incorrect rate, and incomplete recoil — are all measurably reduced by objective feedback devices.

### Cited Findings
- An ILCOR evidence table (EIT-6404) systematic review found pooled improvements in mean depth (15 studies), depth compliance (16), mean rate (17), rate compliance (9), and recoil compliance (10) with feedback devices vs. no feedback. Certainty was rated moderate for mean depth/rate and high for depth compliance — [ILCOR Evidence Table EIT-6404](https://costr.ilcor.org/assets/images/photos/EIT-6404-Feedback-device-during-training_evidence-table.docx)
- Visual feedback produced approximately 78% correct compressions vs. 65% for auditory feedback vs. 45% for no feedback in a crossover simulation study (n=15, small sample caution) — [cited in feedback search results](https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/2)
- Real-time AV feedback in an emergency department simulation study produced significant improvement in compliance with CPR depth and rate targets and "compressions in target" — [BMC Emergency Medicine 2020](https://doaj.org/article/82d68f850cd445959bca83838c8245ca)
- Instructor assessment of compression quality is described as "subjective and suboptimal" — the primary justification for objective device feedback — cited in ILCOR review context
- A Palestinian/Oman study found feedback-based training kept competency for up to 12 months, vs. significant skill decline after 6 months with traditional methods — [Palestinian Medical and Pharmaceutical Journal](https://journals.najah.edu/media/journals/full_texts/1545.pdf)
- A subgroup comparison in the ILCOR review found "no statistically significant interaction between healthcare professionals and laypersons," suggesting feedback benefits apply equally to lay people and professionals — [ILCOR EIT-6404](https://costr.ilcor.org/assets/images/photos/EIT-6404-Feedback-device-during-training_evidence-table.docx)
- Current ILCOR review notes: "no studies evaluated patient survival, clinical performance in resuscitation or cost-effectiveness" — [ILCOR EIT-6404]

### Inferences
- CPR feedback via accelerometer-equipped smartphones (measuring rate and possibly depth) is a practical implementation of evidence-based feedback for lay training.
- The most improvable errors via feedback are depth, rate, and recoil — all measurable through motion sensors.

### Gaps
- No study specifically tested phone accelerometer-based feedback (without a dedicated CPR manikin device) as the feedback mechanism for lay rescuers.
- Long-term retention benefit of training with feedback vs. training without feedback has not been well established in RCTs.

## What Software/Apps Can Realistically Address vs. What Requires Physical Equipment

### Takeaway
Apps can realistically address: knowledge gaps, step-by-step guidance during an event, compression rate feedback (via audio metronome), confidence/willingness, recognition of cardiac arrest, and calling for help. Apps cannot replace physical manikin practice for developing compression depth technique, two-rescue breathing coordination, or tactile skill correction. AR-enhanced phone tools bridge part of this gap.

### Cited Findings
- A 2021 controlled simulation trial (Metelmann et al., n=200) of smartphone CPR guidance found mandatory app use significantly improved correct compression rate and depth, but also significantly delayed the check for breathing, call for help, and first compression — leading to longer total hands-off time. Authors described benefits as "ambivalent" — [PMC8173850, redirected to pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8173850/]
- A 2024 narrative review of app-based CPR support (studies 2014–2024) found "guided CPR applications significantly improve most critical parameters for efficacious resuscitation" but noted delays related to app familiarity as the main limitation — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)
- A 2026 non-randomized RCT (n=481 university students, China) tested a game-based mobile app combined with traditional training vs. traditional training alone for knowledge, skills, and retention — design reported, outcome data not retrieved — [JMIR mHealth 2026](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e78689/PDF)
- A 2011 Japanese smartphone simulation RCT is cited as early evidence that guided apps improve compression delivery — cited in app search results
- AHA and ERC training guidelines recommend metronome/audio feedback for compression rate guidance (implementable via app with no additional hardware) — inferred from ILCOR review context
- A 2018 study of the PocketCPR app found confidence scores improved significantly (50.41 vs. 43.92, p=0.026) with app vs. no app — cited in confidence search results

### Inferences
- The core software-addressable problems are: compression rate (audio/visual metronome), recognition of cardiac arrest, decision support, calling EMS, and knowledge of steps. Depth training requires physical feedback (device or manikin); AR-based phone apps partially address this but with reduced accuracy vs. dedicated devices.
- App familiarization before use is essential — the delay-to-first-compression problem is a training design issue, not a technology limitation.

### Gaps
- No validated study of AR-based CPR training (phone camera + pose estimation for depth estimation) in a lay population was found. This is an emerging area without peer-reviewed evidence yet.
- Long-term real-world outcomes (survival, neurological recovery) from app-guided CPR have not been measured in prospective studies.

## Deliberate Practice and Mastery-Based Learning for Procedural Skills

### Takeaway
Mastery-based learning and deliberate practice improve CPR and resuscitation skill outcomes compared to one-time certification training, but evidence from RCTs is limited and mixed. Observational evidence is more consistently positive. Spaced repetition with feedback is the most evidence-supported design element.

### Cited Findings
- A 2021 scoping review (PMC8244416) included 16 studies (4 RCTs, 12 observational); 12 of 16 showed improved skills with deliberate practice (DP) and/or mastery learning (ML). Only 1 of 4 RCTs found a statistically significant benefit over traditional training — [PMC8244416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8244416/)
- Rapid cycle DP improved chest compression fraction and shortened time to first shock in pediatric simulations — [PMC8244416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8244416/)
- A PALS study found scores improved at 6, 9, and 12 months after a single RCDP session; a booster at 9 months produced no additional benefit at 12 months — [PMC8244416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8244416/)
- 60% of learners did not maintain mastery-level performance at 6 months in one pediatric resuscitation study — [PMC8244416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8244416/)
- The AHA 2018 Resuscitation Education Scientific Statement recommends a mastery model with a minimum passing standard and "overlearning" (training beyond minimum standard) for behaviors likely to decay — [AHA via Heart and Stroke 2018](https://www.heartandstroke.ca/-/media/pdf-files/cpr/new-pdfs/2018highlightsfromtheresuscitationeducationscientificstatement.ashx)
- Advanced cardiac life support simulation produced "high knowledge and skill retention as much as 14 months later" but "has not been widely adopted, possibly due to high time costs" — cited in deliberate practice search results

### Inferences
- Mastery-based, threshold-gated learning modules on a phone (e.g., "pass a compression-rate test before unlocking next module") are aligned with evidence-based pedagogy.
- Spaced repetition micro-sessions (5–10 min every 1–3 months) are more effective than annual recertification but have lower adoption due to perceived inconvenience — the app must make this frictionless.

### Gaps
- No study has tested a mastery-based mobile app for CPR training in a lay Indian population.
- Most DP/ML research is in healthcare professionals (residents, nurses), not lay public — generalizability is uncertain.

## Retention Rates from Standard First-Aid Courses

### Takeaway
Standard first-aid courses produce good immediate post-training competency, but 3-month retention is notably lower and 6-month retention lower still. There is no single agreed-upon percentage; ranges vary widely by skill tested and study design, but multiple reviews confirm substantial decay within 12 months.

### Cited Findings
- A 2012 Resuscitation trial table reported "excellent CPR performance" at: post-training (adults ~51%), 3 months (~37%), 6 months (~30% — not statistically significant from baseline). Infant CPR: 3 months 70%, 6 months 67% — [PMC3600577](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3600577/table/T3) (table data extracted; interpret cautiously)
- A CSA review of ~20 peer-reviewed articles found skill decline beginning in <6 months; refresher training at 6–12 month intervals can maintain skills — [CSA Group](https://dev-www.csagroup.org/article/first-aid-training-and-cpr-skills-retention/)
- A Palestinian Medical and Pharmaceutical Journal systematic review (12 studies) found skills declined significantly after 6 months with traditional methods; feedback-enhanced training maintained competency for up to 12 months — [Najah.edu](https://journals.najah.edu/media/journals/full_texts/1545.pdf)
- Correct compression depth in NRSI data (school-based CPR review) fell from 86.90% immediately post-training to 12.81% at 6–8 months — [PMC12590714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12590714/)
- A 2024 Spanish primary care study reported compression skills declined at 3 months, with progressive loss to 6 months that was "not statistically significant between groups" — [Elsevier Semergen](https://www.elsevier.es/es-revista-medicina-familia-semergen-40-pdf-download-S113835932400087X)
- A 2026 RCT found a brief video refresher at 3 months improved 6-month retention for most skills except AED use vs. no refresher — [TurkMedline](https://turkmedline.net/detay/effectiveness-of-a-video-based-refresher-on-cardiopulmonary-resuscitation-skill-retention-in-college-students-a-single-blind-randomized-controlled-trial/bb9d2345995f62e4/tr/260)

### Inferences
- Even a brief video/app refresher at 3 months has evidence of benefit for 6-month retention — this is implementable in a push-notification-based app feature.
- The skill most vulnerable to decay appears to be compression depth, followed by compression rate. Recognition and calling EMS (procedural knowledge) decay more slowly.

### Gaps
- No standardized "first-aid retention benchmark" across studies — measurement methods, study populations, and skill definitions differ widely.
- No India-specific data on retention after IRC (Indian Resuscitation Council) courses.

## Confidence vs. Actual Skill: The Overconfidence Problem

### Takeaway
Self-rated CPR confidence is a weak predictor of actual performance. Both confidence and skill decay after training, but their trajectories diverge — some studies show confidence outpacing skill, others show it decaying faster. The key actionable finding is that feeling trained does not equate to being able to perform CPR correctly.

### Cited Findings
- A 2013 Israeli study of medical students found that confidence, preparedness, and recollection of CPR were significantly lower 2 years after training vs. 1 year after, with a matched drop in OSCE scores; lowest retention was in safety checking, pulse check, airway opening, rescue breathing, and ventilation technique — [IMA Journal](https://www.ima.org.il/FilesUploadPublic/IMAJ/0/65/32676.pdf)
- A 2018 observational study of 237 BLS-trained people found the least-trained group had lower self-rated confidence AND worse manikin compression quality, suggesting confidence and skill are co-declining but confidence alone cannot identify skill gaps — [Springer IJEM 2018](https://rd.springer.com/article/10.1186%2Fs12245-018-0220-9)
- A 2018 PocketCPR app study found participants were significantly more confident with app guidance (mean 50.41 vs. 43.92 without, p=0.026) but the study did not report whether this confidence matched measured skill improvement — cited in confidence search results
- A dental-student study reported "high level of BLS knowledge and skill confidence" that "did not predict actual competence" — source is an RQI Partners blog post summarizing a study; treat as secondary/marketing-adjacent; the underlying paper was not directly retrieved — [RQI Partners blog](https://rqipartners.com/resources/blog/false-bravado-or-lost-confidence-rqi-bridges-the-gap/)
- An older simulation study found that MDs and RNs performed better than laypersons in assessment, but some in each group initiated CPR without first assessing need — [PMC481810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC481810) (dated study; treat as background only)

### Inferences
- An app that presents confident self-assessment prompts alongside objective performance metrics (compression rate, timing) can reveal the gap between perceived and actual readiness — this is a key design opportunity for Sanjeevani.
- Willingness to act is high in India surveys (87% willing to help), while actual CPR knowledge and skill is low — the gap is specifically in skill, not attitude.

### Gaps
- No Indian study directly compared self-reported CPR confidence with measured compression performance in the same participants.
- Most "overconfidence" research involves medical trainees, not lay public — this specific gap deserves targeted study.

## India-Specific Data: Bystander CPR Rates and Training Gaps

### Takeaway
India has some of the world's lowest bystander CPR rates (1.3%–9.8% in most published studies), almost no free training infrastructure, deeply inadequate EMS coverage, and a complex array of social, legal, and awareness-related barriers. The gap between willingness and capability is large and well-documented.

### Cited Findings
- A 2023 Indian Heart Journal review reported bystander CPR rates in India of 1.3%–9.8%, well below the AHA target of 62% — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- Study-level rates: CARO 2017 (New Delhi) = 1.3% (80 patients); Pandian 2016 = 7.4% (67 patients); Bhat 2021 (Telangana) = 9.8% (205 patients); DA-CPR 2021 and PAROS 2022 each reported 0% — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- OHCA survival in India is estimated at less than 10% — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- AED use in India is extremely low: 1% in Bhat 2021; only 17 of 44 patients in WACAR where data was reported — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- Only 8 of 29 Indian states (28%) and 1 of 7 union territories (14%) have on-site BLS course availability; no free on-site courses exist; courses per 10 million = 0.4 vs. 31.5 in the UK — [PMC7183922](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7183922/)
- No legislation mandates CPR training in India for employees, students, or people obtaining official documents — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- Legal fears and fear of causing harm discourage bystanders; many believe formal BLS training is required before performing CPR — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- A 2026 cross-sectional survey of 583 urban young Indians (18–35) found 73% could not identify the correct chain of survival; only 28% had heard of AED; but 87% were willing to assist an unconscious victim and 94% believed CPR should be taught to the general population — [IJCMPH 2026](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/15152)
- A 2025 rural Karnataka study found 81.4% of 253 adults had inadequate understanding of CPR; education level was the only demographic significantly associated with CPR knowledge — cited in India awareness search results
- A 2025 Kerala study of Kudumbashree workers found 34% had good CPR knowledge; 52.33% thought only healthcare workers should perform CPR; 52% were reluctant to help strangers — [IPHJ Unsoed](https://jos.unsoed.ac.id/index.php/iphj/article/download/12998/6045)
- Karnataka reported a CPR training rate of only 1.5% of the population — cited in India awareness search results
- In WACAR (Telangana), only 24% of patients with symptoms up to an hour before arrest contacted a physician — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- Gender-related hesitancy about performing CPR on women is also reported in India — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)
- No national cardiac arrest registry exists in India as of 2023 — [PMC10568059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10568059/)

### Inferences
- India's barriers are layered: extreme shortage of training access → near-zero trained bystander population → near-zero bystander CPR → <10% OHCA survival. A phone app addresses the first two links in this chain.
- The myth that formal BLS certification is required to perform CPR is a specific, correctable misconception that Sanjeevani's content can directly address.
- High willingness (87%) but low knowledge (73% can't identify chain of survival) means content framing matters more than motivation in India.

### Gaps
- No national epidemiology of OHCA incidence in India — estimates range widely and are based on small regional studies.
- No rigorous study has tracked whether app-based CPR training in India results in increased real-world bystander CPR rates or OHCA outcomes.
- Data on training infrastructure in smaller cities, tier-2 and tier-3 cities, and rural areas are absent.

## Is the Main Unmet Need: Access, Practice Frequency, Feedback Quality, Retention, or Assessment?

### Takeaway
In India specifically, access is the dominant first-order problem (only 0.4 courses per 10M people, concentrated in major cities, no free options). Once solved by a mobile platform, the next problems are retention (rapid decay without refreshers) and feedback quality (learners can't self-assess compression technique). Assessment/overconfidence and practice frequency are secondary but interrelated.

### Cited Findings
- India has 79x fewer BLS courses per capita than the UK and zero free options — access is structurally near-zero — [PMC7183922](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7183922/)
- 81.4% of adults in a Karnataka study had inadequate CPR understanding — the knowledge baseline is near-zero even in educated populations — [cited in India survey results]
- Skill decay begins in <6 months (CSA review), plateaus by 6 months (Resuscitation 2019 review), but current Indian certification, where it exists, operates on 1–2 year cycles — [CSA](https://dev-www.csagroup.org/article/first-aid-training-and-cpr-skills-retention/)
- Feedback devices improve compression quality vs. no feedback (ILCOR EIT-6404); instructor feedback alone is insufficient — [ILCOR EIT-6404](https://costr.ilcor.org/assets/images/photos/EIT-6404-Feedback-device-during-training_evidence-table.docx)
- 73% of urban young Indians cannot identify the chain of survival; only 28% know what an AED is — assessment/measurement is essentially absent at the population level — [IJCMPH 2026](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/15152)

### Inferences
- Sanjeevani should prioritize access as the core product promise (free, phone-based, no instructor required), with feedback quality and retention as the differentiating mechanisms.
- Assessment (showing users whether they actually know/can perform CPR) is a highly underserved function and creates urgency/motivation that pure instructional content does not.
- Practice frequency is addressable via push notification reminders for spaced micro-sessions — this is a software feature with evidence support.

### Gaps
- No single study has directly compared which intervention has the greatest marginal impact on OHCA survival in an Indian context: access expansion, training quality improvement, or refresher frequency.
