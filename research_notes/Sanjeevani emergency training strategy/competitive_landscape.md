# Emergency Skills Training: Competitive Landscape

## Key Question 1: Main First-Aid and CPR Training Apps — What They Offer vs. Claim

### Takeaway
The consumer CPR/first-aid app market is populated by step-by-step reference apps and guided-CPR tools, none of which combines multi-module emergency training (CPR + stroke + allergy) with real-time camera-based feedback, and most have not been independently validated for guideline accuracy.

### Cited Findings

**AHA HeartCode**
- HeartCode is AHA's official blended-learning program: students complete online modules at their own pace, then attend a hands-on skills session with a VAM (video assist manikin) or AHA-authorized instructor. — [AHA HeartCode description via iMedicalApps](https://www.imedicalapps.com/2013/02/learn-cpr-choking-videos-app-androi/)
- Some HeartCode pages still reference 2010 BLS guidelines rather than the current 2020 guidelines; guideline currency should be confirmed before use. — [Search result, via iMedicalApps]
- HeartCode is not an app in the Play Store sense; it is an enterprise web platform sold to hospitals, employers, and training centers. No consumer pricing was found.

**British Red Cross First Aid App**
- Free for Android and iOS. Covers ~20 first-aid skills in the general app; the baby/child variant covers ~17 skills.
- Features: step-by-step guides, instructional videos, interactive quizzes, and offline use. The child app adds animations and a medical-needs toolkit. — [British Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/first-aid-apps)
- Content claims are the publisher's own; no independent clinical validation was found in this research.

**St John Ambulance First Aid App**
- Free, with illustrated guides and voiced instructions; UK protocols and emergency numbers only. Not a substitute for an SJA course per the official listing.
- Launched 2020; limited update activity noted. — [Apptopia listing for com.sja.firstaid](https://apptopia.com/google-play/app/com.sja.firstaid/about)
- "SJA First Aid Fast" is a separate paid/course-linked iPad app requiring a course code; App Store shows only one review, rated 1/5. — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1609616897)

**PulsePoint Respond**
- Free; 3,000,000+ users across 4,950+ communities as of January 2024. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PulsePoint)
- Integrated directly with 911 dispatch centers. When a call-taker determines CPR is needed, the app simultaneously alerts nearby registered bystanders and dispatches EMS. Alerts apply only to cardiac arrests in public locations.
- Users see victim location, nearest AEDs, and can listen to live dispatch audio. Three responder tiers: Public CPR Responder, Registered CPR Responder, Professional Responder. — [PulsePoint factsheet](https://girdwoodfire.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/PulsePoint-Respond-and-AED-Fact-Sheet.pdf)
- Version 4.22 added repeated alert sounds; 4.16 added dispatch notifications by assignment for professional responders. PulsePoint does not include training content — it is purely a dispatch/response coordination tool.

**Me-CPR! (research app)**
- Guided CPR app tested in a 2023 randomized trial. Users matched or outperformed CPR-certified people on most metrics; 50% of compressions rated high-quality vs. 25% for certified responders. However, first-compression time was slower than certified responders. — [PMC11450428 narrative review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)

**Pocket-CPR (Zoll)**
- Uses the phone's accelerometer (not camera). Bystander must hold the phone on the chest while compressing. Improved no-flow time, rate, and hand placement; did not significantly improve depth; some prompts contradicted themselves. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)

**2014 JMIR audit of 61 CPR apps**
- Experts evaluated 46 apps against BLS guidelines; only 13 passed layperson evaluation. Finding: "very few are designed according to current BLS guidelines and offer an acceptable level of usability and hedonic quality for laypersons." — [JMIR 2014 v16i3e89](https://jmir.org/2014/3/e89)

**2018 JMIR quality study**
- Only 1 of 3 tested CPR apps rated above the usability benchmark; authors called for systematic quality control of health apps. — [JMIR mHealth 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e190)

**2024 narrative review (PubMed)**
- Guided CPR apps "significantly improve most critical parameters for efficacious resuscitation." App users achieved performance "comparable to or even better than CPR-certified individuals" in some studies. Key limitation flagged: "familiarity delay" — users may hesitate before opening the app. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)

### Inferences
- No single consumer app covers CPR + stroke FAST + allergic reaction in one coherent training module. This is a structural gap Sanjeevani can own.
- "Free + offline + guideline-current" is the threshold consumer expectation; anything below it is a non-starter.
- Most apps are reference guides or guided-response tools, not training simulators with feedback loops.

### Gaps
- No independent 2024 head-to-head comparison of AHA HeartCode vs. consumer apps on training outcomes was found.
- Pricing for AHA HeartCode enterprise licensing was not publicly available.

## Key Question 2: Feedback-Enabled CPR Manikins — What They Measure That Phones Cannot

### Takeaway
Professional manikins measure compression depth to millimeter precision via direct mechanical sensors, provide true chest recoil feedback, and can accurately assess ventilation volume — capabilities no current phone app can replicate. They cost $120–$1,200+ per unit, creating a major access barrier.

### Cited Findings

**Laerdal Little Anne QCPR**
- Bluetooth-enabled; connects to the QCPR Training app for real-time feedback. Sensors track: compression rate, compression depth, full chest release (recoil), ventilation rate and count.
- Built-in audible clicker provides immediate depth confirmation. One instructor can monitor up to 6 learners simultaneously (42 learners with QCPR Classroom software).
- Supports head tilt, jaw thrust, and nose-pinch ventilation with visible chest rise. — [DefibWarehouse UK product page](https://defibwarehouse.co.uk/shop/training-equipment/laerdal-little-anne-qcpr-manikin/)
- Prices: ~$324 (US, Kiefer); £245 (UK, DefibWarehouse); ~$520 AUD (Australia). — Multiple retailer listings
- Little Anne 2.0: 2 kg, stackable (6 per case), dimensions 64 × 37 × 21.5 cm. — [SJA UK shop](https://shop.sja.org.uk/products/laerdal-little-anne-20-light-skin-training-manikin-with-qcpr)

**Prestan Manikins**
- Entry-level Prestan Adult Ultralite single: ~$120 (US). Prestan Adult 4-pack: ~$455. — [Integris Equipment, eBay listings]
- CPR Rate Monitor provides visual LED feedback on compression rate compliance. Audible compression clicker included on monitor-equipped models.
- Child single with CPR Monitor: ~£184 (UK) vs. Laerdal Little Junior QCPR ~£245 (UK) — Prestan is ~25% cheaper. — [DefibWarehouse UK](https://defibwarehouse.co.uk/product-category/training-equipment/manikins/child-manikins/)
- Laerdal Little Baby QCPR 4-pack: ~$1,179 vs. Prestan Professional Infant 4-pack: ~$659 (US) — significant price gap at the infant level. — [Integris Equipment]

**What manikins measure that phones cannot (by method):**
- **Depth:** Mechanical spring/displacement sensors give millimeter-level accuracy unaffected by surface compliance (phones using accelerometers can overestimate depth on compliant surfaces like foam mattresses). — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)
- **Chest recoil:** Direct spring-return measurement detects incomplete release, which phones cannot reliably detect without being placed directly on the chest.
- **Ventilation volume:** Bellows or lung-compliance simulators measure tidal volume; no phone app currently replicates this.
- **Hand placement:** Some advanced manikins (Resusci Anne) use sensors to detect hand position; phone camera can approximate this but is not reliable.

**Resusci Anne QCPR (advanced model)**
- Laerdal's flagship full-body manikin. Advanced feedback capabilities beyond Little Anne but specific feature list not confirmed from primary sources in this search.

### Inferences
- The "phone as manikin replacement" is explicitly impossible for depth, recoil, and ventilation — three of the five core QCPR metrics. Sanjeevani should frame its value as "practice without a manikin," not "equivalent to a manikin."
- Prestan's price advantage (~17–25% cheaper) makes it the preferred choice for budget-constrained training programs. Laerdal's QCPR ecosystem (app + classroom software) gives it a lock-in advantage in institutional settings.
- Cost per learner for manikin training: a single $324 manikin shared by 10 learners per session still represents ongoing facility, instructor, and scheduling overhead that a phone app eliminates.

### Gaps
- Laerdal's enterprise pricing for QCPR Classroom software was not found.
- Nasco Healthcare manikin pricing was not searched; may fill a mid-range gap.

## Key Question 3: VR/AR Emergency Training Systems

### Takeaway
VR dominates professional emergency training (military, medical students, EMS); systems require expensive hardware (Meta Quest 3, tethered headsets) and cost thousands to tens of thousands per seat. AR is used mainly as a manikin overlay add-on. No consumer-grade phone-AR emergency training app was found.

### Cited Findings

**SimX (enterprise VR)**
- 250+ patient models and environments; 300+ tools and procedures. Supports military programs (VALOR) covering EMS, CBRNE scenarios. Used by US military. — [Search result, January 2024]
- Target users: military, hospital simulation labs, nursing schools. No pricing found; enterprise licensing assumed.

**STEP-VR (academic study, XR)**
- Tested on 529 senior medical students over two years; used an untethered headset. Authors concluded XR-based training "could help prepare future physicians for emergency care." Published BMC Medical Education, September 2024. — [PubMed 39252064](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39252064/)
- Required institution-grade headsets; not consumer-available.

**North American Rescue Virtual Patient Immersive Trainer**
- Used by US Air Force 163d Medical Group (January 2024). Enables training on multiple patients and trauma scenarios in quick succession. Acquired via innovation center, not commercial off-the-shelf. — [US AF news photo caption]

**RCT comparing manikin vs. manikin+VR vs. manikin+AR (2025)**
- Statistically significant knowledge differences post-intervention between groups; VR group highest for knowledge. Results not significant for satisfaction. Authors: mixed-reality integration with physical manikins is feasible. — [Digibug.ugr.es / Healthcare April 2025 RCT]
- Target population: healthcare students / trainees.

**Tactical medicine VR thesis (TU Wien, 2025)**
- Runs on Meta Quest 3. Covers triage, bleeding control, communication. Academic prototype, not commercially available. — [TU Wien repository](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/216548)

**General VR evidence review (PMC)**
- Effective VR features identified: interactive UI, task performance recording, monitoring and feedback, scenario editor, realism and presence, multi-user collaboration.
- Gaps: "tools and studies related to the multiplayer experience, haptic feedback, and evaluation of user's manual skills are still limited." — [PMC8987970](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8987970/)

### Inferences
- All identified VR systems are for medical students, military, or institutional EMS — zero systems target general public or student bystanders who own only a phone.
- Hardware cost (Meta Quest 3: ~$500+; enterprise VR kits: $5,000–$50,000) creates an insurmountable access barrier for Sanjeevani's target audience.
- The "AR overlay on a manikin" use case is niche; the more scalable gap is AR guidance in training without any manikin, which no product currently fills for consumers.

### Gaps
- Pricing for SimX enterprise licensing was not found.
- No consumer-priced ($0–$50) phone-AR first-aid training app was identified; this appears to be a genuine market gap.

## Key Question 4: Phone-Based Camera CPR Feedback Apps

### Takeaway
Camera-based CPR feedback exists as research prototypes and one instructor tool; no publicly available consumer app uses phone camera for real-time compression feedback to a lay rescuer. The technical challenge is feasible but unsolved in a deployed product.

### Cited Findings

**QCPR Cam-App 2.0 (Norwegian research group)**
- Camera detects inactivity and compressions; measures compression rate with real-time feedback to bystander and remote dispatcher over the web.
- Average compression rate detection error: 2.7 compressions per minute; summary CPR parameters averaged 4.5% error.
- Phone lies flat on the ground beside the patient (not handheld). Authors found "no other published work or products using the smartphone camera to measure compression rate" at time of publication.
- Limitation: earlier version failed with long loose hair and bystanders moving around the scene. — [UiS repository](https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/handle/11250/2420348); [PMC6277120](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6277120/)

**Frequency-analysis algorithm (research)**
- Finds dynamic region of interest in video frames; evaluates power spectral density via FFT over sliding windows to detect compression rate.
- Tested with different people, backgrounds, lighting, and phone placements against a recording manikin for ground truth. Not a skeleton/pose approach.
- Not a deployed product. — [PMC4982121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4982121/)

**CCF Monitor (iOS instructor app)**
- Camera pointed at manikin; automatically detects compressions and pauses. Shows live chest compression fraction and four key metrics. Designed for BLS/ACLS instructor use, not lay bystander use. — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6760084371)

**Pocket-CPR (Zoll) — accelerometer, not camera**
- Accelerometer-based; user holds phone while compressing. Improved rate and no-flow time; did not significantly improve depth. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)

### Inferences
- The gap between "research prototype" and "consumer app" for camera-based CPR feedback has not been closed by any major vendor.
- CCF Monitor demonstrates commercial viability for the instructor use case. The lay-rescuer use case (real emergency + camera guidance) remains unsolved.
- For training (not real emergencies), a camera detecting compression on a pillow or hard surface is a technically achievable differentiator for Sanjeevani.

### Gaps
- No deployed Android app using phone camera for CPR compression feedback to laypersons was found.
- Depth estimation accuracy from a static camera (without accelerometer) was not measured in any found study; this is a key unknown for Sanjeevani's design.

## Key Question 5: AI and Pose-Estimation Training Research

### Takeaway
Two peer-reviewed studies (2023, 2024) demonstrate proof-of-concept pose estimation for CPR assessment; chest-proximity team metrics show moderate accuracy while arm-angle metrics diverge significantly from expert ratings. Deep learning classifiers show promise but are trained on small, single-center datasets.

### Cited Findings

**Weiss et al. (2023), Advances in Simulation**
- 91 healthcare providers in simulated BLS; OpenPose + stereo webcams + MATLAB 3D triangulation (no body markers).
- Two metrics: arm straightness and chest-to-chest distance (team proximity).
- Arm angle: pose estimation classed 13.2% as straight-armed; experts classed 90.6%. Disagreement: 77.3% (κ = 0.03 — essentially chance). Expert inter-rater agreement κ = 0.88.
- Chest-to-chest distance: disagreement 20.7% (κ = 0.57 — moderate agreement). Expert inter-rater κ = 0.93.
- Authors: pose estimation "assessed learners' arm angles in more detail and their chest-to-chest distance comparably to expert ratings." — [PMC10105636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10105636/); [Advances in Simulation DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/872dfa54a1dd43a5890f84711a74278c)

**Constable et al. (2024/2025), Advances in Health Sciences Education**
- Automated BLS assessment tool using pose estimation + deep learning quality classifier.
- Training data: video footage, expert quality ratings (2 raters), self-reported confidence, demographics.
- Accepted August 2024; published February 2025.
- Demonstrates AI-graded CPR quality from video, not just rate/depth metrics. — [Hubertshum.com publication page](https://hubertshum.com/pbl_ahse2024cpr.htm); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/2785989)

**Limitations common to both studies:**
- Small single-center datasets; not validated cross-site.
- Weiss: arm angle κ = 0.03, calling into question pose estimation's accuracy for that specific metric.
- Neither study tested whether pose estimation feedback improved training outcomes vs. control.
- Neither produced a deployable consumer app.

### Inferences
- Pose estimation for CPR is scientifically validated as partially feasible — specifically for team positioning (κ = 0.57) and qualitative depth/quality classifiers.
- Arm straightness from a single phone camera is technically unreliable with current methods; the multi-camera stereo setup used by Weiss is not mobile-compatible.
- Sanjeevani should target pose estimation for posture/body position cues (are the user's arms straight? are they kneeling correctly?) rather than precise depth measurement, where phones cannot match mechanical sensors.

### Gaps
- No study tested MediaPipe (Google's mobile-optimized pose library) specifically for CPR feedback on a single phone camera at training distances (~1–2 m).
- No published accuracy benchmark exists for single-camera, single-phone CPR assessment on Android at typical training conditions.

## Key Question 6: Commoditized Features vs. Genuinely Hard Problems

### Takeaway
Step-by-step video guides, quizzes, and metronomes are fully commoditized. Real-time accurate depth measurement from a phone, multi-emergency AI assessment, and adaptive personalized feedback are genuinely hard and unimplemented in consumer products.

### Cited Findings

**Commoditized (many products implement these):**
- Step-by-step illustrated and video guides for CPR, choking, burns, stroke: British Red Cross, St John Ambulance, iSAVElives, virtually all first-aid apps. — Multiple sources above
- Offline access: British Red Cross app. — [redcross.org.uk/first-aid/first-aid-apps](https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/first-aid-apps)
- Metronome / compression rate indicator (100–120 cpm target): multiple apps including MediCode, CCF Monitor. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)
- AED locator maps: PulsePoint, iSAVElives. — [Wikipedia/PulsePoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PulsePoint)
- Multiple-choice quizzes: British Red Cross app, AHA HeartCode. — Multiple sources

**Genuinely hard (no deployed consumer solution found):**
- Real-time depth feedback from phone accelerometer or camera: technically difficult; accelerometer requires phone placement on chest; camera depth estimation at low resolution is unsolved for consumers. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)
- Full chest recoil feedback without mechanical sensor: no phone-based solution found. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)
- Ventilation (rescue breath) volume feedback: only manikins with lung simulators can measure this.
- Pose/form quality scoring (arm straightness, body weight transfer): Weiss 2023 shows κ = 0.03 with two cameras; single phone is harder. — [PMC10105636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10105636/)
- Stroke FAST detection using phone camera: FAST.AI research app achieved ~100% facial asymmetry detection and ~67% arm weakness detection in acute stroke patients screened by neurologists — not validated for lay self-use; not yet publicly released as of 2023. — [Neuronewsinternational.com](https://neuronewsinternational.com/smartphone-app-identify-stroke-symptoms-isc-2023/)
- Multi-emergency training in a single adaptive app (CPR + stroke + allergy): no product found.
- Gamified scenario training with AI-graded performance in first-aid: Constable 2024 is research-only; no commercial consumer product. — [Hubertshum.com](https://hubertshum.com/pbl_ahse2024cpr.htm)

### Inferences
- Sanjeevani's potential technical moat is in the combination of (a) multi-emergency module breadth and (b) any form of camera/sensor-based practice feedback — even approximate — that no existing consumer app provides.
- Quality control on first-aid app content is a known market failure (2014 JMIR, 2018 JMIR); a medically reviewed, guideline-anchored app is itself a differentiator.

### Gaps
- No price or feature list was found for Zoll's full PocketCPR product suite beyond the research app used in studies.
- No study was found measuring app performance for lay users in India specifically, which is Sanjeevani's primary context.

## Key Question 7: Underserved User Segments

### Takeaway
Children/schoolchildren, older adults, low-health-literacy users, and communities without EMS infrastructure are the most consistently named underserved groups in the literature, but few products target them explicitly.

### Cited Findings

**Children and schoolchildren:**
- A 2024/2026 Brazil study designed a serious game for BLS for schoolchildren; bystander CPR rates and public proficiency remain low, and children are an important future responder group. — [PMC11481818](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11481818/)
- A 2026 qualitative China study on gamified CPR kits for children and parents flagged child-friendly design and personalized feedback as underserved design needs. — [JMIR Games 2026](https://games.jmir.org/2026/1/e88145)
- Most app studies test young adults; "older adults struggled to sustain CPR" and were underrepresented. — [PMC11450428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450428/)

**Low-health-literacy / accessibility:**
- A student design project (AidReady) identified: "most first aid apps use long paragraphs & complex language, leaving children, seniors, and people with disabilities unable to act." — [AidReady paper, WPI](https://users.wpi.edu/~cxu5/docs/AidReady.pdf)
- Proposed features (not yet deployed): picture/video display, text-to-speech, offline access.

**Communities without formal EMS:**
- PulsePoint explicitly requires 911 integration and public-location alerts; it does not serve rural areas without dispatch integration or private settings.
- Sanjeevani's India context: emergency response infrastructure is geographically uneven; no app in the competitive set explicitly targets emerging market / low-connectivity users.

**Professional refreshers:**
- A Danish gamified app study targeted already-certified people needing refreshers. Post-certification retention is a separate underserved use case. — [BMJ EMJ 36:653](https://emj.bmj.com/content/36/11/653)

### Inferences
- No competitor targets "first-time Indian urban bystander with a smartphone but no CPR training" as a specific persona. Sanjeevani can own this segment.
- Offline functionality, local-language support, and low-bandwidth design would be structural differentiators in emerging market contexts.
- Gamification is widely recommended in research for retention and re-engagement, but no leading consumer app currently executes it well for first aid.

### Gaps
- No published study on first-aid app usage or effectiveness in India was found.
- Language localization for Indian regional languages in first-aid apps was not studied in any found source.

## Summary: Sanjeevani's Exploitable Gaps

1. **No multi-emergency app:** Zero products combine CPR + stroke FAST + allergy training in one app for general consumers. Sanjeevani is first-to-market for this combination.

2. **No camera-based consumer training feedback:** CCF Monitor (instructor use, iOS) and research prototypes exist, but no Android app offers camera-based CPR practice feedback to lay users. Even approximate feedback (posture, rate from motion) would be unique.

3. **No emerging market / India-specific product:** All major apps target US/UK audiences; PulsePoint requires 911 integration. Sanjeevani can own low-bandwidth, offline-first, Indian-context design.

4. **Gamification gap:** Research consistently recommends gamification for retention; no leading first-aid app executes it at quality. Sanjeevani's scenario + quiz structure can differentiate here.

5. **Stroke + allergy modules are genuinely uncharted in consumer apps:** FAST.AI is unpublished/unreleased; no allergy recognition training app was found. Both modules have real clinical stakes and no consumer competitor.

6. **Price/access gap vs. manikins:** A $324 manikin serves a training center; Sanjeevani serves anyone with a $150 Android phone. This is the single largest access differentiator.
